Family Political Discussion & Youth Voting Participation

How the frequency of political discussion within families is associated with participation in Kazakhstan's March 2026 constitutional referendum among university students eligible to vote

Presentation #1 · Positivist Research Design

1 · The Empirical Puzzle

From Political Talk to Political Action

What we know
Families are political socialization environments. McIntosh, Hart & Youniss (2007): adolescents who discuss politics and current events more frequently tend to show higher political knowledge, greater intention to vote, and stronger civic outcomes.
But there is a puzzle
Šerek & Umemura (2015): longitudinal data on 223 first-time voters in the Czech Republic. Peer discussion was associated with later participation; parental discussion did not show the same direct relationship, though it predicted more peer discussion.
Core puzzle: does talking about politics frequently within the family correspond to actually voting among young adults?

3 · Epistemological Positioning

Positivism: Claim → Evidence From My Design

① Observable & measurable
Principle: phenomena can be operationalized.
In my design: discussion = Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often; participation = Did not participate / Participated. Prior research uses frequency scales and binary voting measures.
② Testable relationship
Principle: defined variables can be compared.
In my design: I compare levels of family political discussion with reported referendum participation to test whether higher discussion frequency is associated with a higher likelihood of participation.
③ Theory → hypothesis → evidence
Principle: theory yields testable expectations.
In my design: political socialization theory → directional hypothesis → empirical evidence can support or fail to support the hypothesis.
4 · Contribution & Kazakhstan Context

Why This Case?

Kazakhstan research
Baizhumakyzy et al. (2025): studied political participation among 350 young people from five universities in Kazakhstan, emphasizing political culture, civic responsibility and youth engagement.
International research
Existing studies connect family political socialization and political discussion with political knowledge, civic engagement and electoral participation, although findings about direct parental effects are mixed.
My contribution: I bring these literatures together by examining whether frequency of family political discussion is associated with participation among eligible university students in the specific context of Kazakhstan's March 2026 constitutional referendum.

6 · What the Literature Tells Us

An Unresolved Relationship

Family matters
Jennings et al. (2009): politicized homes strengthen transmission. McIntosh et al. (2007): frequent discussion linked to civic outcomes.
But…
Šerek & Umemura (2015): parental political discussion did not directly predict voting intentions or subsequent participation; peer discussion did. Parent discussion, however, predicted more frequent peer discussion.
Open question: does family political discussion have a measurable association with participation among university students in Kazakhstan?
7 · Theory & Hypotheses

Political Socialization & Social Learning

Frequent family political discussion
↓
Repeated exposure to political information, opinions, cues
↓
Greater political awareness / knowledge
↓
Political issues become more salient
↓
Greater likelihood of participation

A proposed mechanism, not an established causal finding from my data.

8 · Hypothesis & Variables

9 · Operationalization & Alternatives

How Could I Test This?

IV question (proposed)
“Before the March 2026 referendum, how often did you discuss politics with members of your family?”
(Politics includes discussions about government, elections or referendums, political leaders, public policies, and current political issues.)
1 Never · 2 Rarely · 3 Sometimes · 4 Often · 5 Very often
DV question (proposed)
“Did you participate in the March 2026 constitutional referendum?”
0 = No · 1 = Yes
Association ≠ causation. Alternative explanation: pre-existing political interest could increase both family political discussion and participation; interested youth may also start conversations with parents (McIntosh et al.).
